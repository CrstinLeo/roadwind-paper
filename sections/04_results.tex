\subsection{Synoptic stations overestimate the roadside wind by more than half}
\label{sec:res-ladder}
Without correction, the reference stations overestimate the mean wind at the roadside stations by \noLadRawBiasAbs\,\% in Norway and \seLadRawBiasAbs\,\% in Sweden (median over station pairs less than \SI{80}{km} apart; \noPairsEighty{} pairs in Norway and \sePairsEighty{} in Sweden; Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). Each step of the correction ladder removes a different part of this gap, and the parts differ between the landscapes. In forested, low-lying western Sweden, height and roughness conversion reduce the median pair error from \seLadRaw{} to \seLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$, because the synoptic stations stand in open terrain and the roadside stations in forest. In mountainous Norway, where both networks have the same nominal sensor height and much of the difference is caused by terrain, the roughness conversion changes the error only from \noLadRaw{} to \noLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$. The site factors slightly increase the error of individual pairs in both countries; they are designed for the combined estimate, where they make the reference stations comparable (Section~\ref{sec:res-site}). The roadside exposure model gives the largest single reduction, to \noLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Norway and \seLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden, and removes most of the bias (\noLadExpoBias\,\% and \seLadExpoBias\,\%).

\subsection{The corrected estimate is skilful out of time and out of station}
\label{sec:res-skill}
Combining all corrected reference stations gives a roadside estimate whose error is far below that of the nearest station, for stations and periods that were not used for fitting (Table~\ref{tab:skill}, Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}). In Norway the median station error of the hourly wind speed falls from \noCvRaw{} to \noCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ (\noCvRed\,\%), and \noCvBetter{} of \noTrv{} stations improve; in Sweden it falls from \seCvRaw{} to \seCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ (\seCvRed\,\%), with \seCvBetter{} of \seTrv{} stations improved. The reduction holds in every month (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}c) and is similar in the leaf-on (\noOnRed\,\%) and leaf-off season (\noOffRed\,\%); absolute errors are larger in winter, when the wind is stronger. The mean bias is small (median over stations \noCvRbias\,\% in Norway and \seCvRbias\,\% in Sweden). Like any regression estimate, the combined estimate is conditionally biased at the extremes: for hours with an estimate of at least \SI{8}{m.s^{-1}} the observed wind is on average \noCvHighObs\,\% (Norway) and \seCvHighObs\,\% (Sweden) lower than estimated, and at the rare observed speeds above \SI{10}{m.s^{-1}} the corrected estimate is no better than the nearest station (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}b). Wind direction improves less, from \noCvDirRaw{} to \noCvDir$^\circ$ in Norway, where valleys and fjords turn the wind strongly, and from \seCvDirRaw{} to \seCvDir$^\circ$ in Sweden. Compared with the configuration that was the starting point of the analysis (a single tree scale of \SI{200}{m}, a speed--shelter interaction, direction-dependent site factors and a \SI{60}{km} combination), the final model reduces the error by a further \noRefSixDiff~$\pm$~\noRefSixSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Norway and \seRefSixDiff~$\pm$~\seRefSixSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden.

At a sheltered road in a valley (a), the nearest reference station is an exposed mountain site whose wind is several times the roadside wind; the correction brings the estimate to within a fraction of a metre per second. At a typical station (b), the corrected estimate follows the timing and size of a storm. At the station where the correction fails most (c), an open bridge near the coast, the roadside wind is as strong as at the nearest reference station, an exposed island station \SI{5}{km} away. The correction removes the high site factor of that island station and averages it with inland stations, and none of the exposure predictors can raise the estimate above the regional wind; the model has no term for exposure over open water.

\subsection{Forest shelters the road at two distinct scales}
\label{sec:res-model}
The roadside exposure model attributes the remaining difference to four predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}, Table~\ref{tab:coefs}). The binned partial residuals follow the fitted linear terms in both countries, so the log-linear form is adequate. Three effects agree in sign between the countries. The roadside wind increases with the distance to the forest edge in units of tree height (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}c), as expected for the sheltered zone behind an edge, and increases relative to the reference wind when the regional wind is strong (d), possibly because the surface layer is better mixed in strong wind. The forest fraction within \SI{1}{km} reduces the roadside wind in both countries (b), strongly so in Norway, where it varies between open plateaus and coasts and forested valleys: an increase of the forest fraction by 0.4 reduces the roadside wind by \noFarFortyPct\,\% at the same distance from the edge. The near-tree share has opposite signs (a): in Sweden trees within \SI{100}{m} reduce the roadside wind, whereas in Norway, with the forest fraction of the landscape already in the model, stations with trees close to the road have higher wind than expected. The two tree terms are correlated, and in Norway near trees often mark settlements and cuttings in otherwise open terrain; the near-tree coefficient should therefore be read together with the forest fraction. Within each country the coefficients are stable: all terms keep their sign, and their size changes little, when one of the time blocks is left out (Table~\ref{tab:coefs}).

The wind direction at the roadside is turned towards the road axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}e,f). At Swedish stations with trees along the road, the deflection changes sign with the angle $\alpha$ between the reference wind and the road, as in a channel, with an amplitude of about \ang{20}; at open Swedish stations the deflection is nearly independent of $\alpha$. Both countries show a mean anticlockwise turn of about \ang{14}. In Norway, open stations also show channelling, which is caused by terrain rather than trees; an additional channelling term without tree scaling, however, did not reduce the out-of-sample direction error (\noDirOpen~$\pm$~\noDirOpenSE$^\circ$ in Norway, \seDirOpen~$\pm$~\seDirOpenSE$^\circ$ in Sweden), because the terrain-induced deflection differs from station to station.

\subsection{Each reference station has its own persistent exposure}
\label{sec:res-site}
The site factors differ by up to a factor of two from the network in either direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:site}): from \noSfMin{} to \noSfMax{} in Norway. Lighthouses, islands and exposed coastal or mountain sites read high; inland airports and valley stations read low. The factors are estimated from a year (Norway) or several years (Sweden) before the test period and are applied unchanged, so they are a persistent property of the site. Without them the combination of stations would average incompatible measurements: in Sweden, omitting the site factors increased the error by \seSiteNone~$\pm$~\seSiteNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$. In Norway the effect of omitting them is uncertain (\noSiteNone~$\pm$~\noSiteNoneSE), and a single factor per station is better than direction-dependent factors (\noSiteSector~$\pm$~\noSiteSectorSE).

\subsection{Combining stations matters more than any single correction}
\label{sec:res-sens}
Figure~\ref{fig:sens} shows the change in cross-validated error in Norway when one choice is varied around the final model. Restricting the combination to stations within \SI{20}{km} or \SI{40}{km} increases the error by \noKmTwenty{} and \noKmForty~m\,s$^{-1}$, more than removing any physical step; even \SI{80}{km} is worse than using all stations (\noKmEighty~$\pm$~\noKmEightySE). Weights $d^{-1}$ to $d^{-1.5}$ are best. The landscape forest fraction is the most important exposure term (\noFarNone~$\pm$~\noFarNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ without it), and it works better in all directions than in the upwind sector (\noFarUp~$\pm$~\noFarUpSE). The near-tree radius hardly matters in Norway (\SI{50}{m}: \noTreeFifty, \SI{200}{m}: \noTreeTwoHundred, \SI{500}{m}: \noTreeFiveHundred~m\,s$^{-1}$). The roughness conversion (\noNoRough), a speed--shelter interaction (\noInter) and the cross-road wind component (\noCross) change the error by less than \SI{0.01}{m.s^{-1}}. None of the \noTerrN{} combinations of terrain indices (upwind shelter index, topographic position, relief) added to the final model reduced the error by more than its standard error (changes between \noTerrMin{} and \noTerrMax~m\,s$^{-1}$). In Sweden, where the scans were made around the reference configuration, the parameters of the roughness conversion (footprint length \SIrange{25}{2000}{m}, blending height \SIrange{30}{150}{m}, $z_0/h$ between 0.05 and 0.15) changed the error by at most \sePhysMax~m\,s$^{-1}$, and the two shelter scales were confirmed independently (Fig.~\ref{fig:twoscale}): near trees within \SIrange{50}{100}{m} and the forest fraction within about \SI{1}{km}, whereas intermediate radii of \SIrange{300}{500}{m} add nothing.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Exposure decorrelates along the road over about 250\,m}{Exposure decorrelates along the road over about 250 m}}
\label{sec:res-along}
The exposure features change quickly along the road, and almost identically in the two countries (Fig.~\ref{fig:along}a). Their correlation with the value at the station is \alongHundredMin--\alongHundredMax{} at \SI{100}{m}, \alongTwoFiftyMin--\alongTwoFiftyMax{} at \SI{250}{m} and \alongKmMin--\alongKmMax{} at \SI{1}{km} along the road. Replacing the point features of the Swedish stations by averages over a road segment leaves the error unchanged for segments of \SI{200}{m} (\seSegTwo~$\pm$~\seSegTwoSE~m\,s$^{-1}$); segments of \SI{500}{m} and \SI{1}{km} tend to increase it, by about one standard error (\seSegFive~$\pm$~\seSegFiveSE{} and \seSegThousand~$\pm$~\seSegThousandSE; Fig.~\ref{fig:along}b). At the roadside stations the exposure varies little at the scale of a segment, so the correlation length, not this test, is the main argument for short segments.

\subsection{The model form transfers, part of the coefficients does not}
\label{sec:res-transfer}
With the coefficients fitted in the other country, the median station error is \noWithSe~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Norway (own coefficients \noCvMed, raw \noCvRaw) and \seWithNo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden (own \seCvMed, raw \seCvRaw). The transferred model keeps a clear advantage over the nearest station in Sweden but only a small one in Norway, mainly because the Norwegian forest-fraction coefficient is \noFarRatio{} times the Swedish one. The model fails at a few Norwegian stations even with its own coefficients (\noWorseN{} of \noTrv; MAE raw $\rightarrow$ corrected in m\,s$^{-1}$): \noWorseList. These are a bridge, an exposed causeway over the sea, a mountain pass, a deep valley with strong channelling, and a station next to a reference station---situations that are rare in the training data or in which the nearest station is already representative.

\subsection{At vehicle height, wind direction limits the drag estimate}
\label{sec:res-vehicle}
Against the van-mounted anemometer (Table~\ref{tab:vehicle}, Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}), the station-based estimate has a mean absolute error of \vehModel~m\,s$^{-1}$ over all \vehN{} segment passages, smaller than that of the nearest reference station (\vehRefraw) and the nearest road weather station (\vehRoadraw), but larger than that of the MET Nordic analysis (\vehMetnordic). The overall advantage over the nearest stations comes from Frøya, where both nearest stations are more than \vehFroyaKmMin~km away and represent opposite exposures: the road weather station is sheltered and the reference station exposed. The model, which combines all stations and applies the exposure of the segment, corrects most of both errors there. At Dovre, where the road passes close to both a reference and a road weather station, the nearest road weather station is the best estimate and the model the least accurate one. Most of its error arises on the evening of 31 October, when all estimates overestimate the van-measured wind (model \vehDovreEveModel\,\%, MET Nordic \vehDovreEveMetnordic\,\%, nearest road weather station \vehDovreEveRoadraw\,\%).

The difference in drag error between the station-based estimate and MET Nordic lies in the wind direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}d). Replacing the station-based direction by the MET Nordic direction reduces the drag error from \vehDragModel{} to \vehDragModelSpeedMnDir\,\%, the error of MET Nordic itself (\vehDragMetnordic\,\%), whereas replacing the station-based speed by the MET Nordic speed changes it little (\vehDragMnSpeedModelDir\,\%). With the measured direction, the two speeds give similar drag errors (\vehDragModelSpeedTrueDir{} and \vehDragMnSpeedTrueDir\,\%).

\subsection{Air drag at the roadside stations}
\label{sec:res-drag}
For a tractor--semitrailer passing the roadside stations in both directions at \SI{80}{km.h^{-1}}, the median station error of the air drag falls from \noDragRaw{} to \noDragModel\,\% in Norway and from \seDragRaw{} to \seDragModel\,\% in Sweden when the corrected estimate replaces the nearest reference station (Table~\ref{tab:skill}, Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}d). The improvement is largest at the stations with the largest raw errors: the upper tail of the distribution, which dominates the uncertainty of range predictions, is removed in both countries.
