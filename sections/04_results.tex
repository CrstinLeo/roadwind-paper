\subsection{Reference stations overestimate the roadside wind by about half}
\label{sec:res-ladder}
Without correction, the nearest reference station overestimates the mean wind at the roadside stations by \noSpdBiasAbsRaw\,\% (median over stations), and a reference station less than \SI{80}{km} away by \noLadRawBiasAbs\,\% (median over the \noPairsEighty{} pairs; Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). The correction ladder shows where this difference comes from. Because both Norwegian networks measure at the same nominal height, height conversion does nothing, and roughness conversion changes the median pair error only from \noLadRaw{} to \noLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$: in mountainous terrain the difference between station and road is not mainly one of surface roughness. The site factors increase the error and the bias of individual pairs (\noLadSiteBias\,\%), because they remove the high exposure of coastal reference stations whose wind happens to resemble that of exposed roadside sites; their role is to make the stations comparable before they are combined (Section~\ref{sec:res-site}). The roadside exposure model gives the largest reduction, to \noLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$, and removes most of the bias (\noLadExpoBias\,\%). In the Swedish development region, where synoptic stations stand in open fields and roads run through forest, the overestimate is larger (\seLadRawBiasAbs\,\%) and height and roughness conversion already remove about a quarter of the pair error.

\subsection{The corrected wind is skilful out of sample and beats the simple alternatives}
\label{sec:res-skill}
Combining all corrected reference stations gives a roadside estimate whose error is far below that of the nearest station, for stations and periods that were not used for fitting (Table~\ref{tab:estimators}, Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}). The median station error of the hourly wind speed falls from \noCvRaw{} to \noCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ (\noCvRed\,\%), and \noCvBetter{} of \noTrv{} stations improve. The reduction holds in every month (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}d) and in the leaf-on (\noOnRed\,\%) and leaf-off season (\noOffRed\,\%). It is not an artefact of model selection. With the scanned choices repeated on 2024 alone, two alternatives were slightly better than the final configuration, a near-tree radius of \SI{50}{m} (\noSelTreeD~$\pm$~\noSelTreeSE~m\,s$^{-1}$) and a weight exponent of 1 (\noSelPowD~$\pm$~\noSelPowSE), both within about one standard error, and all others were worse; we kept the final configuration for consistency with the development region. Fitted on 2024, it has a median error of \noHoldMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ in 2025 against \noHoldRaw~m\,s$^{-1}$ for the nearest station (\noHoldRed\,\%).

The simple alternatives remove part of the error, but less. Multiplying the nearest station by one constant (\noRescConst, learned out of sample) removes its bias but reduces the median error only to \noSpdResc~m\,s$^{-1}$, because the overestimate differs from station to station. The nearest other road weather station, at a median distance of \noRoadKm~km, has a median error of \noSpdRoad~m\,s$^{-1}$: neighbouring roadside sites are as different from each other as from the reference stations. The MET Nordic analysis, evaluated on every third hour, overestimates the roadside wind by \noSpdBiasMnhMn\,\% and has a median error of \noSpdMnhMn~m\,s$^{-1}$, against \noSpdMnhCor~m\,s$^{-1}$ for the corrected estimate on the same hours; per station, the corrected estimate is better at \noPairCorMnBetter{} of \noTrv{} stations, and the mean difference (\noPairCorMn~$\pm$~\noPairCorMnSE~m\,s$^{-1}$) is not significant. Once its bias is removed with one constant learned from the road weather network, MET Nordic reaches \noSpdMnhMnResc~m\,s$^{-1}$, and with the exposure model on top \noSpdMnhMnExp~m\,s$^{-1}$, the lowest error of all estimators; the corrected estimate is worse than either by about \noPairCorMnExp~m\,s$^{-1}$ on average (better at \noPairCorMnExpBetter{} stations). For the wind direction, MET Nordic is the best estimate (median error \noDirMnhMn$^\circ$ against \noDirMnhCor$^\circ$ for the corrected estimate on the same hours), and the corrected direction improves on the nearest station only from \noDirRaw{} to \noDirCor$^\circ$, because valleys and fjords turn the wind strongly.

The corrected estimate is not calibrated over its whole range (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}b). For low estimates the observed wind is higher than estimated (ratio \noRelLow{} below \SI{1}{m.s^{-1}}), and for high estimates lower: for hours with an estimate of at least \SI{8}{m.s^{-1}} the observed wind is on average \noRelEight\,\% lower than estimated, and \noRelHigh{} times the estimate above \SI{\noRelHighLo}{m.s^{-1}}. The reason is the back-transformation from the logarithmic model: the scatter of $\ln(U_j/U_3)$ decreases with the wind speed, so that a single smearing factor, dominated by the many light-wind hours, over-corrects strong winds. A recalibration learned out of sample removes this conditional bias (ratios \noRelCalMin--\noRelCalMax{} above \SI{1}{m.s^{-1}}) but does not reduce the MAE (Table~\ref{tab:estimators}), because the MAE is dominated by the scatter, not by the bias. Viewed from the observation, all estimators underestimate the strongest winds. For observed speeds above \SI{15}{m.s^{-1}} (\noStrongObsN{} station-hours with all estimators), the MAE is \noStrongObsCor~m\,s$^{-1}$ for the corrected estimate (mean error \noStrongObsMeCor), \noStrongObsRoad{} for the nearest road station and \noStrongObsRaw{} for the nearest station, which does better here only because it overestimates the wind in general. MET Nordic, which resolves storms with a dynamical model, is clearly best in these hours (\noStrongObsMn~m\,s$^{-1}$, mean error \noStrongObsMeMn; with the exposure model \noStrongObsMnExp; Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}c).

\subsection{The landscape forest fraction controls roadside shelter}
\label{sec:res-model}
The roadside exposure model attributes the remaining difference to four predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}, Table~\ref{tab:coefs}). The binned partial residuals follow the fitted linear terms, so the log-linear form is adequate. Only two terms are clearly determined across stations. The forest fraction within \SI{1}{km} is the dominant one (station-bootstrap interval of $b_4$: \noBFarLo{} to \noBFarHi): an increase of the forest fraction by 0.4, almost its full range at the stations, reduces the roadside wind by \noFarFortyPct\,\% (\noFarFortyLo--\noFarFortyHi\,\%). Applied on top of MET Nordic, the same term is weaker ($b_4={}$\,\noHybFar), because the analysis already resolves part of the landscape effect. The roadside wind also increases relative to the reference wind when the regional wind is strong ($b_3$: \noBUnetLo{} to \noBUnetHi). Fitted separately, this term is close to zero on summer days (\noUnetDay) but clearly positive in summer nights (\noUnetNight) and in winter (\noUnetWinter). This points to stable stratification: in light wind under stable conditions the sheltered roadside is decoupled from the flow aloft, and the decoupling weakens as the wind increases. The distance to the forest edge ($b_2$: \noBXhLo{} to \noBXhHi) and the near-tree share ($b_1$: \noBTreeLo{} to \noBTreeHi) are not determined in Norway: their intervals include zero, the two predictors are correlated ($r=\noCorrTreeXh$; largest variance inflation factor \noVifMax), and removing the near-tree term entirely changes the cross-validated error by \noNoTree~$\pm$~\noNoTreeSE~m\,s$^{-1}$. By the selection rule of Section~\ref{sec:validation} it would be dropped in Norway. In the Swedish forest roads, however, the near-tree share has a negative coefficient and is needed (\seNoTree~$\pm$~\seNoTreeSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ without it), and we keep it, as an exception to the rule, in the common model form; without it, the Norwegian energy results are unchanged (skill \enSsNoCorNoTree, correlation of the site penalties \enRoadCorrNoCorNoTree). The object layer counts bridge decks and buildings as obstacles, and the bridges are among the most exposed sites. Refitting without the \noBridgeN{} bridge and causeway stations (\noBridgeList) changes the forest-fraction coefficient from \noBFar{} to \noBFarNoBridge{} and the near-tree coefficient from \noBTree{} to \noBTreeNoBridge: the forest effect remains, but these few exposed sites contribute to its size. All terms keep their sign when one of the eight time blocks is left out (Table~\ref{tab:coefs}).

The forest fraction may partly act as a proxy for terrain: in central Norway forest grows in sheltered valleys, while plateaus and coasts are open. Explicit terrain indices did not reduce the out-of-sample error (Section~\ref{sec:res-sens}), and with three of them entered before the forest fraction, its coefficient changes from \noFarFinal{} to \noFarWithTerrain, so that most of its effect is not explained by these indices.

The wind direction at the roadside is turned towards the road axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}e,f). Where trees line the road, the deflection changes sign with the angle $\alpha$ between the reference wind and the road, as in a channel, with an amplitude of about \ang{20}; this is clearest in the Swedish forest roads (e). Both regions also show a mean anticlockwise turn ($d_0\approx-\ang{14}$). At open Norwegian roadside stations the observed mean turn is \noTurnAll$^\circ$. It is larger at night (\noTurnNight$^\circ$) than by day (\noTurnDay$^\circ$), which agrees with the stronger backing of the near-surface wind under stable stratification, but it is also larger in strong (\noTurnHighU$^\circ$) than in light reference wind (\noTurnLowU$^\circ$), which points to deflection by terrain. The data therefore do not isolate a single cause; the magnetic declination in the region, about \ang{4} east, would turn the wind the other way. In Norway, open stations also show channelling by terrain (f); an additional channelling term without tree scaling did not reduce the out-of-sample direction error (\noDirOpen~$\pm$~\noDirOpenSE$^\circ$), because the terrain-induced deflection differs from station to station.

\subsection{Site factors make the reference stations comparable}
\label{sec:res-site}
The site factors range from \noSfMin{} to \noSfMax{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:site}): lighthouses, islands and exposed coastal or mountain sites read up to twice the network wind, inland airports and valley stations down to half of it. They are estimated from the year before the test period and applied unchanged. Factors estimated in the same way from 2024 and from 2025 correlate with them at $r=\noSfPersistA$ and \noSfPersistB, so they are a persistent property of the sites. A single factor per station is better than direction-dependent factors (\noSiteSector~$\pm$~\noSiteSectorSE~m\,s$^{-1}$). In Norway, however, the gain from the factors is not significant (\noSiteNone~$\pm$~\noSiteNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ without them), whereas in the Swedish development region, where three lighthouse stations read about twice the network wind, omitting them increases the error by \seSiteNone~$\pm$~\seSiteNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Combining stations matters more than any single correction}
\label{sec:res-sens}
Figure~\ref{fig:sens} shows the change in cross-validated error when one choice is varied around the final model. Restricting the combination to stations within \SI{20}{km} or \SI{40}{km} increases the error by \noKmTwenty{} and \noKmForty~m\,s$^{-1}$, more than removing any physical step; even \SI{80}{km} is worse than using all stations (\noKmEighty~$\pm$~\noKmEightySE). Weights $d^{-1}$ to $d^{-1.5}$ are best. The landscape forest fraction is the most important exposure term (\noFarNone~$\pm$~\noFarNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ without it); within \SI{300}{m} (\noFarThree~$\pm$~\noFarThreeSE) or \SI{500}{m} (\noFarFive~$\pm$~\noFarFiveSE) it is less effective than within \SI{1}{km}, it works better in all directions than in the upwind sector (\noFarUp~$\pm$~\noFarUpSE), and adding a separate ring within \SI{300}{m} does not help (\noRing~$\pm$~\noRingSE). The near-tree radius does not matter (\SI{50}{m}: \noTreeFifty, \SI{300}{m}: \noTreeThreeHundred, \SI{500}{m}: \noTreeFiveHundred~m\,s$^{-1}$). The roughness conversion (\noNoRough), a speed--shelter interaction (\noInter) and the cross-road wind component (\noCross) change the error by less than \SI{0.01}{m.s^{-1}}. None of the \noTerrN{} combinations of terrain indices added to the final model reduced the error by more than its standard error (changes between \noTerrMin{} and \noTerrMax~m\,s$^{-1}$).

\subsection{Wind raises the air-drag energy at the station sites by about a tenth}
\label{sec:res-energy}
For a tractor--semitrailer at \SI{80}{km.h^{-1}} the still-air drag requires \enEzero~kWh per \SI{100}{km}. At the \noDirOkN{} roadside stations with direction coverage, the observed wind raises this on average by \enAddNo\,\% (95\,\% station-bootstrap interval \enAddNoLo--\enAddNoHi\,\%; \enAddKwhNo~kWh/100\,km; Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}). Most of the increase comes from crosswind, which raises the drag area through the yaw angle in both driving directions (crosswind alone: \enCrossNo\,\%); head- and tailwind alone contribute \enHeadNo\,\%, because they largely cancel over both directions. The size of the penalty depends on how the \SI{10}{m} wind is brought to vehicle height (Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}a): a sensor at \SI{6}{m} instead of \SI{10}{m} gives \enBandNoSix\,\%, roughness lengths of 0.01 to \SI{0.3}{m} give \enBandNoZLow{} to \enBandNoZHigh\,\%, a displacement height of \SI{2}{m} \enBandNoDisp\,\%, and no height reduction \enBandNoNoRed\,\%. Turbulence adds little (\enBandNoTiLow--\enBandNoTiHigh\,\% for intensities of 0.15--0.3), whereas the yaw model matters most: Model~1 of \citet{Askerdal2024crosswind} gives \enAddNoMOne\,\%.

The penalty differs between station sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}b): from \enRoadMinNo\,\% to \enRoadPninetyNo\,\% at the 90th percentile and \enRoadMaxNo\,\% at the most exposed site, i.e.\ \enRoadPninetyKwhNo~kWh/100\,km or more at one site in ten. The largest penalties occur at bridges, causeways and on the Dovre mountain pass, the kind of sites at which road weather stations are placed; the distribution therefore describes the station network, not the road network. (The mean over sites, \enRoadMeanNoTrue\,\%, differs slightly from the pooled mean of \enAddNo\,\% because sites have different numbers of hours.) The relative penalty decreases with speed (\enAddNoSixty\,\% at \SI{60}{km.h^{-1}}, \enAddNoNinety\,\% at \SI{90}{km.h^{-1}}). In \enStrongFracNo\,\% of the one-way passes the wind changes the air-drag energy by at least \SI{10}{\percent}, and in \enLargeFracNo\,\% by at least \SI{20}{\percent}.

The estimators differ sharply (Table~\ref{tab:estimators}, Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}c). Ignoring the wind underestimates the long-term air-drag energy of the median site by \enBiasNoNone\,\%. The nearest reference station overestimates it by \enBiasNoRaw\,\%: it roughly doubles the mean penalty of the sites (\enRoadMeanNoRaw\,\% instead of \enRoadMeanNoTrue\,\%), ranks the sites poorly (correlation \enRoadCorrNoRaw, interval \enRoadCorrNoRawLo{} to \enRoadCorrNoRawHi), and hour by hour it is worse than no wind at all (pooled skill \enSsNoRaw; positive at \enStPosNoRaw{} of \enNoStations{} sites). Rescaling the nearest station by one constant removes the bias but not the hourly error (skill \enSsNoResc, interval \enSsNoRescLo{} to \enSsNoRescHi), and the nearest other road station does no better (\enSsNoRoad; correlation of the site penalties \enRoadCorrNoRoad). The corrected wind removes most of the bias (\enBiasNoCor\,\%), has a positive pooled skill (\enSsNoCor, interval \enSsNoCorLo{} to \enSsNoCorHi) and captures part of the variation between sites (correlation \enRoadCorrNoCor, interval \enRoadCorrNoCorLo{} to \enRoadCorrNoCorHi; mean absolute error of the site penalty \enRoadMaeNoCor{} percentage points). The pooled values are, however, dominated by the most exposed sites. Site by site, the skill is positive at \enStPosNoCor{} of \enNoStations{} sites (median \enStSsNoCor), and the hourly energy error is lower than when ignoring the wind at only \enStGainNoCor{} sites. Pooled, its hourly error is \enMaeGainNo\,\% below that of ignoring the wind (\enMaeNoCor{} vs \enMaeNoNone~kWh/100\,km; difference \enDNoCorNoneMae, interval \enDNoCorNoneMaeLo{} to \enDNoCorNoneMaeHi) and \enMaeGainRawNo\,\% below that of the nearest station; in the windiest quarter of hours, selected by the regional reference wind, the reduction relative to ignoring the wind is \enCondGainNo\,\% (\enCondNoCor{} vs \enCondNoNone~kWh/100\,km).

On the hours with MET Nordic, the raw analysis has a pooled skill of \enMnhNoSsMn{} (interval \enMnhNoSsMnLo{} to \enMnhNoSsMnHi), lower than the corrected wind (\enMnhNoSsCor), but it ranks the sites about as well (correlation \enMnhNoCorrMn{} against \enMnhNoCorrCor), because its better direction compensates for its overestimate of the speed. Rescaled by one constant it reaches a skill of \enMnhNoSsMnResc{} (corrected minus rescaled: \enMnhDNoCorMnRescSkill, interval \enMnhDNoCorMnRescSkillLo{} to \enMnhDNoCorMnRescSkillHi), and with the exposure model \enMnhNoSsMnExp{} and the lowest energy error of all estimators (\enMnhNoMaeMnExp{} against \enMnhNoMaeCor~kWh/100\,km for the corrected wind; difference \enMnhDNoMnExpCorMae, interval \enMnhDNoMnExpCorMaeLo{} to \enMnhDNoMnExpCorMaeHi), with positive skill at \enMnhNoStPosMnExp{} sites. The exposure model thus adds a small further improvement to the rescaled analysis (energy MAE \enMnhDNoMnExpMnRescMae, interval \enMnhDNoMnExpMnRescMaeLo{} to \enMnhDNoMnExpMnRescMaeHi~kWh/100\,km). These conclusions hold for speeds between 60 and \SI{90}{km.h^{-1}} (skill of the corrected wind \enSsNoCorSixty--\enSsNoCorNinety), with Model~1 (skill: nearest station \enSsNoRawMOne, corrected \enSsNoCorMOne) and with other conversions to vehicle height (no height reduction: \enBandSsNoNoRedRaw{} and \enBandSsNoNoRedCor; $z_0=\SI{0.3}{m}$ with $d=\SI{2}{m}$: \enBandSsNoDispRaw{} and \enBandSsNoDispCor). In the Swedish development region, where the corrected direction is better, the penalty is similar (\enAddSe\,\%) and the corrected wind is more skilful (\enSsSeCor, positive at \enStPosSeCor{} of \enSeStations{} sites).

\subsection{At vehicle height, the wind direction limits the drag estimate}
\label{sec:res-vehicle}
In the eight reference hours with van measurements (Table~\ref{tab:vehicle}, Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}), the observed wind raised the air-drag energy along the drives by \enVehPen\,\% on average. No station-based estimate predicted the hourly energy better than ignoring the wind: the mean absolute error was \enVehNone~kWh/100\,km without wind, \enVehRef{} with the nearest reference station, \enVehRoad{} with the nearest road weather station and \enVehCor{} with the corrected wind (interval \enVehCorLo--\enVehCorHi), which overestimated the energy by \enVehBiasCor\,\%. Only the MET Nordic analysis was better (\enVehMn, interval \enVehMnLo--\enVehMnHi; bias \enVehBiasMn\,\%). With eight hours, each weighted equally although they contain between 7 and 120 segment passages, the intervals overlap widely; the comparison is a case study.

Exchanging speed and direction between the sources shows where the difference comes from (Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}c). Giving the corrected estimate the MET Nordic direction reduces its energy error from \vehECorCor{} to \vehECorMn~kWh/100\,km, whereas giving it the MET Nordic speed leaves it at \vehEMnCor; with the measured direction the two speeds give \vehECorTrue{} and \vehEMnTrue. Most of the difference is therefore due to the direction, the rest to the speed. The wind direction of a station network, even of a nearby station, does not represent the flow channelled along a mountain road. The speed itself is also uncertain at vehicle height: where the van passed within \SI{100}{m} of a road weather station (\vehNearSeg{} segments in \vehNearHours{} hours), it measured \vehNearRatio{} times the station wind converted to \SI{3.4}{m} (\vehNearRatioMin--\vehNearRatioMax{} in single hours), i.e.\ about the station wind itself (factor \vehNearFactor{} instead of 0.81). Converting the corrected estimate with the local roughness profile instead of the open-terrain factor changes its energy error to \enVehCorLoc~kWh/100\,km.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Exposure decorrelates along the road over a few hundred metres}{Exposure decorrelates along the road over a few hundred metres}}
\label{sec:res-along}
For vehicle studies the question is over which length a road segment has one exposure. The exposure features change quickly along the road (Fig.~\ref{fig:along}a): their correlation with the value at the station is \alongHundredMin--\alongHundredMax{} at \SI{100}{m}, \alongTwoFiftyMin--\alongTwoFiftyMax{} at \SI{250}{m} and \alongKmMin--\alongKmMax{} at \SI{1}{km} along the road, almost identically in both regions. Averaging the near-tree features of the Swedish stations over a road segment of \SI{200}{m} increases the error slightly (\seSegTwo~$\pm$~\seSegTwoSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ relative to the point value), and segments of \SI{500}{m} and \SI{1}{km} more (\seSegFive{} and \seSegThousand; Fig.~\ref{fig:along}b).

\subsection{The model form transfers, part of the coefficients does not}
\label{sec:res-transfer}
With the coefficients fitted in the Swedish development region, the median station error in Norway is \noWithSe~m\,s$^{-1}$ (own coefficients \noCvMed, nearest station \noCvRaw); conversely, the Norwegian coefficients give \seWithNo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden (own \seCvMed, nearest station \seCvRaw). The transferred model keeps only a small advantage in Norway, mainly because the Norwegian forest-fraction coefficient is \noFarRatio{} times the Swedish one. Even with its own coefficients the model fails at \noWorseN{} of \noTrv{} Norwegian stations (MAE nearest $\rightarrow$ corrected in m\,s$^{-1}$): \noWorseList. These are a bridge, an exposed causeway over the sea, a mountain pass, a deep valley with strong channelling, and a station next to a reference station---situations that are rare in the training data or in which the nearest station is already representative.
