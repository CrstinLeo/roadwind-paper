\begin{abstract}
Wind changes the air drag of heavy trucks, but the roadside wind is rarely known: weather stations measure at \SI{10}{m} in open terrain, whereas roads run near the ground through forest and valleys. Using only open data, we correct station wind to roadside weather stations and ask what this changes in estimates of the air-drag energy of a tractor--semitrailer. In central Norway (\noRef{} reference and \noTrv{} roadside stations, 23 months), the nearest reference station overestimates the mean roadside wind by \noSpdBiasAbsRaw\,\%. Height and roughness conversion, a site factor per station, an exposure model dominated by the forest fraction within \SI{1}{km}, and the combination of all stations reduce the median hourly speed error from \noCvRaw{} to \noCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ out of time and out of station (\noHoldMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ in 2025 with all choices made on 2024), better than a rescaled nearest station, the nearest other roadside station and the MET Nordic analysis. At \SI{80}{km.h^{-1}} the observed roadside wind raises the air-drag energy at the station sites by \enAddNo\,\% (95\,\% interval \enAddNoLo--\enAddNoHi\,\%), mostly through crosswind; plausible height conversions give \enBandNoLo--\enBandNoHi\,\%, a stronger yaw model \enAddNoMOne\,\%. The nearest station roughly doubles the penalty and predicts hourly energy worse than ignoring the wind (skill \enSsNoRaw); the corrected wind has a skill of \enSsNoCor{} and captures part of the variation between sites (correlation \enRoadCorrNoCor), but overestimates strong winds. At vehicle height, in eight hours on two roads, no station-based estimate predicted the energy better than ignoring the wind; the MET Nordic analysis did, mainly through its wind direction.
\end{abstract}
