\subsection{Correction ladder}
\label{sec:res-ladder}
Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} follows the hourly wind speed of all pairs of a roadside and a reference station less than \SI{80}{km} apart (\noPairsEighty{} pairs in Norway, \sePairsEighty{} in Sweden) through the steps of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ladder}, with the exposure model evaluated leave-one-station-out. Without correction the reference stations overestimate the mean roadside wind by more than half (median pair bias \noLadRawBias\,\% in Norway and \seLadRawBias\,\% in Sweden). The physical steps behave differently in the two landscapes. In Sweden, height and roughness conversion reduce the median pair error from \seLadRaw{} to \seLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$, because the synoptic stations stand in open terrain and the roadside stations in forest. In Norway, where both networks share the same sensor height and much of the difference is caused by terrain, the roughness conversion changes the error only from \noLadRaw{} to \noLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$. The site factors slightly increase the median pair error in both countries; they are designed for the combined estimate, where they make the reference stations comparable (Section~\ref{sec:res-sens}). The roadside exposure model gives the largest reduction in both countries, to \noLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Norway and \seLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden, and removes most of the bias (\noLadExpoBias\,\% and \seLadExpoBias\,\%).

\subsection{Skill out of time and out of station}
\label{sec:res-skill}
Table~\ref{tab:skill} summarises the cross-validated skill of the final model, in which every estimate is made for a time block and a roadside station that were not used for fitting. In Norway the median station error of the hourly wind speed falls from \noCvRaw~m\,s$^{-1}$ (nearest reference station) to \noCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$, a reduction of \noCvRed\,\%, and \noCvBetter{} of \noTrv{} stations improve. The reduction is similar in the leaf-on (\noOnRed\,\%) and the leaf-off season (\noOffRed\,\%); absolute errors are larger in winter, when the wind is stronger. In Sweden the median error falls from \seCvRaw{} to \seCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ (\seCvRed\,\%), with \seCvBetter{} of \seTrv{} stations improved. Wind direction improves less: from \noCvDirRaw{} to \noCvDir$^\circ$ in Norway, where valleys and fjords turn the wind strongly, and from \seCvDirRaw{} to \seCvDir$^\circ$ in Sweden. Compared with the reference configuration that was the starting point of the analysis (a single tree scale of \SI{200}{m}, a speed--shelter interaction, direction-dependent site factors and a \SI{60}{km} combination), the final model reduces the error by \noRefSixDiff~$\pm$~\noRefSixSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Norway and \seRefSixDiff~$\pm$~\seRefSixSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden. Out of time and out of station the mean bias is small (median over stations \noCvRbias\,\% in Norway and \seCvRbias\,\% in Sweden). Like any regression estimate, the combined estimate is conditionally biased at the extremes: for hours with an estimated wind of at least \SI{8}{m.s^{-1}} the observed roadside wind is \noCvHighObs\,\% (Norway) and \seCvHighObs\,\% (Sweden) lower than estimated.

\subsection{Roadside exposure model}
\label{sec:res-model}
Table~\ref{tab:coefs} gives the coefficients in the two countries. Within each country they are stable: all terms keep their sign, and their size changes little, when one of the time blocks is left out. Between the countries, three terms agree in sign---the distance to the forest edge, the regional wind speed and the landscape forest fraction---and the direction model has similar coefficients in both countries ($d_0\approx\ang{-14}$, $|d_1|=\ang{25}$--\ang{34}), i.e.\ a mean backing of the roadside wind relative to the reference network and a turning towards the road axis that grows with the near-tree share. The landscape forest fraction is by far the strongest predictor in Norway, where it varies between open mountain plateaus and forested valleys. The near-tree term has opposite signs: in Sweden trees within \SI{100}{m} reduce the roadside wind, whereas in Norway, with the forest fraction of the landscape already in the model, stations with trees close to the road have slightly higher wind than expected. The two tree terms are correlated, and in Norway near trees often mark road cuttings and settlements in otherwise open terrain; the near-tree coefficient should therefore not be interpreted in isolation.

\subsection{Sensitivity to length scales and model choices}
\label{sec:res-sens}
Figure~\ref{fig:sens} shows the change in cross-validated error in Norway when one choice is varied around the final model. The combination of reference stations matters most: using only stations within \SI{20}{km} or \SI{40}{km} increases the error by \noKmTwenty{} and \noKmForty~m\,s$^{-1}$; even \SI{80}{km} is worse than using all stations (\noKmEighty~$\pm$~\noKmEightySE). Weights $d^{-1}$ to $d^{-1.5}$ are best. The landscape forest fraction is essential (\noFarNone~$\pm$~\noFarNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ without it), and taking it in all directions is better than in the upwind sector (\noFarUp~$\pm$~\noFarUpSE). The near-tree radius hardly matters in Norway (\SI{50}{m}: \noTreeFifty, \SI{200}{m}: \noTreeTwoHundred, \SI{500}{m}: \noTreeFiveHundred~m\,s$^{-1}$). One site factor per reference station is better than direction-dependent factors (\noSiteSector~$\pm$~\noSiteSectorSE), while omitting the site factors gives an uncertain result (\noSiteNone~$\pm$~\noSiteNoneSE). The roughness conversion (\noNoRough), a speed--shelter interaction (\noInter) and the cross-road wind component (\noCross) change the error by less than \SI{0.01}{m.s^{-1}}. None of the \noTerrN{} combinations of terrain indices (upwind shelter index, topographic position, relief) added to the final model reduced the cross-validated error by more than its standard error (changes between \noTerrMin{} and \noTerrMax~m\,s$^{-1}$). In Sweden, where the scans were made around the reference configuration, omitting the site factors increased the error by \seSiteNone~$\pm$~\seSiteNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$, and the parameters of the roughness conversion (footprint length \SIrange{25}{2000}{m}, blending height \SIrange{30}{150}{m}, $z_0/h$ between 0.05 and 0.15) changed it by at most \sePhysMax~m\,s$^{-1}$. The two shelter scales were confirmed independently in Sweden (Fig.~\ref{fig:twoscale}): near trees within \SIrange{50}{100}{m} and the forest fraction within about \SI{1}{km}, whereas intermediate radii of \SIrange{300}{500}{m} add nothing.

\subsection{Along-road correlation of exposure}
\label{sec:res-along}
The exposure features change quickly along the road, and almost identically in the two countries (Fig.~\ref{fig:along}a). Their correlation with the value at the station is \alongHundredMin--\alongHundredMax{} at \SI{100}{m}, \alongTwoFiftyMin--\alongTwoFiftyMax{} at \SI{250}{m} and \alongKmMin--\alongKmMax{} at \SI{1}{km} along the road. Replacing the point features of the Swedish stations by averages over a road segment leaves the error unchanged for segments of \SI{200}{m} (\seSegTwo~$\pm$~\seSegTwoSE~m\,s$^{-1}$); segments of \SI{500}{m} and \SI{1}{km} tend to increase it, by about one standard error (\seSegFive~$\pm$~\seSegFiveSE{} and \seSegThousand~$\pm$~\seSegThousandSE; Fig.~\ref{fig:along}b). At the roadside stations the exposure varies little at the scale of a segment, so the correlation length, not this test, is the main argument for short segments.

\subsection{Transfer between the countries}
\label{sec:res-transfer}
With the coefficients fitted in the other country, the median station error is \noWithSe~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Norway (own coefficients \noCvMed, raw \noCvRaw) and \seWithNo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden (own \seCvMed, raw \seCvRaw). The transferred model keeps a clear advantage over the nearest station in Sweden but only a small one in Norway, mainly because the Norwegian forest-fraction coefficient is much larger than the Swedish one.

\subsection{Vehicle height}
\label{sec:res-vehicle}
Against the van-mounted anemometer (Table~\ref{tab:vehicle}, Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}), the station-based estimate has a mean absolute error of \vehModel~m\,s$^{-1}$ over all \vehN{} segment passages, smaller than that of the nearest reference station (\vehRefraw) and the nearest road weather station (\vehRoadraw), but larger than that of the MET Nordic analysis (\vehMetnordic). The overall advantage over the nearest stations comes from Frøya, where both nearest stations are more than \vehFroyaKmMin~km away and represent opposite exposures: the road weather station is sheltered and the reference station exposed. The model, which combines all stations and applies the exposure of the segment, corrects most of both errors there. At Dovre, where the road passes close to both a reference and a road weather station, the nearest road weather station is the best estimate and the model the least accurate one. Most of its error arises on the evening of 31 October, when all estimates overestimate the van-measured wind (model \vehDovreEveModel\,\%, MET Nordic \vehDovreEveMetnordic\,\%, nearest road weather station \vehDovreEveRoadraw\,\%).

The difference in drag error between the station-based estimate and MET Nordic lies in the wind direction. Replacing the station-based direction by the MET Nordic direction reduces the drag error from \vehDragModel{} to \vehDragModelSpeedMnDir\,\%, the error of MET Nordic itself (\vehDragMetnordic\,\%), whereas replacing the station-based speed by the MET Nordic speed changes it little (\vehDragMnSpeedModelDir\,\%). With the measured direction, the two speeds give similar drag errors (\vehDragModelSpeedTrueDir{} and \vehDragMnSpeedTrueDir\,\%).

\subsection{Air drag at the roadside stations}
