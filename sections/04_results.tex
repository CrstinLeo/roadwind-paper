\subsection{Synoptic stations overestimate the roadside wind by more than half}
\label{sec:res-ladder}
Without correction, the reference stations overestimate the mean wind at the roadside stations by \noLadRawBiasAbs\,\% (median over the \noPairsEighty{} station pairs less than \SI{80}{km} apart; Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). The correction ladder shows where this difference comes from. Because both Norwegian networks measure at the same nominal height, height conversion does nothing, and roughness conversion changes the median pair error only from \noLadRaw{} to \noLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$: in mountainous terrain the difference between station and road is not mainly one of surface roughness. The site factors slightly increase the error of individual pairs; they are designed for the combined estimate, where they make the reference stations comparable (Section~\ref{sec:res-site}). The roadside exposure model gives the largest reduction, to \noLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$, and removes most of the bias (\noLadExpoBias\,\%). In the Swedish check region, where synoptic stations stand in open fields and roads run through forest, the overestimate is larger (\seLadRawBiasAbs\,\%) and height and roughness conversion already remove about a quarter of the pair error (\seLadRaw{} to \seLadRough~m\,s$^{-1}$); the exposure model then reduces it to \seLadExpo~m\,s$^{-1}$.

\subsection{The corrected wind is skilful out of time and out of station}
\label{sec:res-skill}
Combining all corrected reference stations gives a roadside estimate whose error is far below that of the nearest station, for stations and periods that were not used for fitting (Table~\ref{tab:skill}, Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}). The median station error of the hourly wind speed falls from \noCvRaw{} to \noCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ (\noCvRed\,\%), and \noCvBetter{} of \noTrv{} stations improve. The reduction holds in every month (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}c) and is similar in the leaf-on (\noOnRed\,\%) and leaf-off season (\noOffRed\,\%). The mean bias is small (median over stations \noCvRbias\,\%). Like any regression estimate, the combined estimate is conditionally biased at the extremes: for hours with an estimate of at least \SI{8}{m.s^{-1}} the observed wind is on average \noCvHighObs\,\% lower than estimated, and at observed speeds above \SI{10}{m.s^{-1}} the corrected estimate is no better than the nearest station (Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}b). Wind direction improves less, from \noCvDirRaw{} to \noCvDir$^\circ$, because valleys and fjords turn the wind strongly. In the Swedish check region the error falls from \seCvRaw{} to \seCvMed~m\,s$^{-1}$ (\seCvRed\,\%, \seCvBetter{} of \seTrv{} stations).

The time series in Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries} show what the numbers mean. At a sheltered road in a valley (a), the nearest reference station is an exposed mountain site whose wind is several times the roadside wind; the correction brings the estimate to within a fraction of a metre per second. At a typical station (b), the corrected estimate follows the timing and size of a storm. At the station where the correction fails most (c), an open bridge near the coast, the roadside wind is as strong as at the nearest reference station, an exposed island station \SI{5}{km} away. The correction removes the high site factor of that island station and averages it with inland stations, and none of the exposure predictors can raise the estimate above the regional wind; the model has no term for exposure over open water.

\subsection{Forest shelters the road at two distinct scales}
\label{sec:res-model}
The roadside exposure model attributes the remaining difference to four predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}, Table~\ref{tab:coefs}). The binned partial residuals follow the fitted linear terms, so the log-linear form is adequate. The roadside wind increases with the distance to the forest edge in units of tree height (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}c), as expected for the sheltered zone behind an edge, and increases relative to the reference wind when the regional wind is strong (d), possibly because the surface layer is better mixed in strong wind. The forest fraction within \SI{1}{km} is the dominant term (b): an increase of the forest fraction by 0.4 reduces the roadside wind by \noFarFortyPct\,\% at the same distance from the edge. With the landscape forest fraction in the model, stations with trees close to the road have higher wind than expected (a); in Norway near trees often mark settlements and road cuttings in otherwise open terrain, and the two tree terms are correlated, so the near-tree coefficient should be read together with the forest fraction. All terms keep their sign, and their size changes little, when one of the eight time blocks is left out (Table~\ref{tab:coefs}). The Swedish check region shows the same effects of the edge distance, the regional wind and the forest fraction, but a negative near-tree effect, as expected for roads in continuous forest.

The wind direction at the roadside is turned towards the road axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}e,f). Where trees line the road, the deflection changes sign with the angle $\alpha$ between the reference wind and the road, as in a channel, with an amplitude of about \ang{20}; this is clearest in the Swedish forest roads (e). Both regions show a mean anticlockwise turn of about \ang{14}. In Norway, open stations also show channelling, which is caused by terrain rather than trees (f); an additional channelling term without tree scaling, however, did not reduce the out-of-sample direction error (\noDirOpen~$\pm$~\noDirOpenSE$^\circ$), because the terrain-induced deflection differs from station to station.

\subsection{Each reference station has its own persistent exposure}
\label{sec:res-site}
The site factors range from \noSfMin{} to \noSfMax{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:site}): lighthouses, islands and exposed coastal or mountain sites read up to twice the network wind, inland airports and valley stations down to half of it. The factors are estimated from the year before the test period and applied unchanged, so they are a persistent property of the site. A single factor per station is better than direction-dependent factors (\noSiteSector~$\pm$~\noSiteSectorSE~m\,s$^{-1}$). In Norway the effect of omitting the factors is uncertain (\noSiteNone~$\pm$~\noSiteNoneSE), whereas in the Swedish check region, where three lighthouse stations read about twice the network wind, omitting them increased the error by \seSiteNone~$\pm$~\seSiteNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Combining stations matters more than any single correction}
\label{sec:res-sens}
Figure~\ref{fig:sens} shows the change in cross-validated error when one choice is varied around the final model. Restricting the combination to stations within \SI{20}{km} or \SI{40}{km} increases the error by \noKmTwenty{} and \noKmForty~m\,s$^{-1}$, more than removing any physical step; even \SI{80}{km} is worse than using all stations (\noKmEighty~$\pm$~\noKmEightySE). Weights $d^{-1}$ to $d^{-1.5}$ are best. The landscape forest fraction is the most important exposure term (\noFarNone~$\pm$~\noFarNoneSE~m\,s$^{-1}$ without it), and it works better in all directions than in the upwind sector (\noFarUp~$\pm$~\noFarUpSE). The near-tree radius hardly matters (\SI{50}{m}: \noTreeFifty, \SI{200}{m}: \noTreeTwoHundred, \SI{500}{m}: \noTreeFiveHundred~m\,s$^{-1}$). The roughness conversion (\noNoRough), a speed--shelter interaction (\noInter) and the cross-road wind component (\noCross) change the error by less than \SI{0.01}{m.s^{-1}}. None of the \noTerrN{} combinations of terrain indices (upwind shelter index, topographic position, relief) added to the final model reduced the error by more than its standard error (changes between \noTerrMin{} and \noTerrMax~m\,s$^{-1}$). The Swedish scans confirm the two shelter scales independently, with no effect of intermediate radii of \SIrange{300}{500}{m} (\ref{app:sweden}).

\subsection{Wind costs a truck about a tenth of its air-drag energy, and the cost is road-specific}
\label{sec:res-energy}
For a tractor--semitrailer at \SI{80}{km.h^{-1}} the still-air drag requires \enEzero~kWh per \SI{100}{km}. The observed roadside wind raises this on average by \enAddNo\,\% (\enAddKwhNo~kWh/100\,km; Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}a). Almost all of the increase comes from crosswind (\enCrossNo\,\%), which raises the drag area through the yaw angle in both driving directions; head- and tailwind contribute only \enHeadNo\,\% because they largely cancel. In a single hour, the wind changes the air-drag energy of a one-way pass by between \enHourQfiveNo{} and +\enHourQninetyfiveNo\,\% (5th to 95th percentile), and that of a round trip by up to +\enRtQninetyfiveNo\,\%. The long-term penalty differs strongly between roads (Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}b): from \enRoadMinNo\,\% at sheltered roads to \enRoadPninetyNo\,\% at the 90th percentile and \enRoadMaxNo\,\% at the most exposed road, i.e.\ \enRoadPninetyKwhNo~kWh/100\,km or more for one road in ten. The relative penalty decreases with speed (\enAddNoSixty\,\% at \SI{60}{km.h^{-1}}, \enAddNoNinety\,\% at \SI{90}{km.h^{-1}}), but the absolute penalty increases (Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}d). The Swedish check region gives similar values (mean \enAddSe\,\%, up to \enRoadMaxSe\,\% at single roads).

The three estimates of this cost differ sharply. Ignoring the wind underestimates the long-term air-drag energy of the median road by \enBiasNoNone\,\%. The nearest reference station overestimates it by \enBiasNoRaw\,\%: it roughly doubles the mean road penalty (\enRoadMeanNoRaw\,\% instead of \enRoadMeanNoTrue\,\%) and ranks the roads poorly (correlation with the observed road penalty \enRoadCorrNoRaw). Hour by hour, the nearest-station wind is worse than no wind at all: its skill score relative to ignoring the wind is \enSsNoRaw, and its mean absolute energy error is \enMaeNoRaw~kWh/100\,km compared with \enMaeNoNone{} for no wind. The corrected wind removes most of the bias (\enBiasNoCor\,\%), reproduces the road penalties (correlation \enRoadCorrNoCor, mean \enRoadMeanNoCor\,\%, mean absolute error \enRoadMaeNoCor{} percentage points) and has a positive skill score (\enSsNoCor). Its advantage is largest in the hours that matter most for range: in hours in which the wind changes the air-drag energy by at least \SI{10}{\percent}, its error is \enStrongNoCor~kWh/100\,km against \enStrongNoNone{} without wind and \enStrongNoRaw{} with the nearest station (Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}c). Averaged over all hours, its gain over ignoring the wind is small in Norway (\enMaeNoCor{} vs \enMaeNoNone~kWh/100\,km), because the remaining error of the wind direction controls the crosswind; in the Swedish check region, where the direction is better captured, the gain is large (\enMaeSeCor{} vs \enMaeSeNone, skill \enSsSeCor). These conclusions hold for all speeds between 60 and \SI{90}{km.h^{-1}} (skill \enSsNoCorSixty--\enSsNoCorNinety) and for the stronger crosswind sensitivity of Model~1 of \citet{Askerdal2024crosswind}, with which the mean penalty is \enAddNoMOne\,\%, the nearest-station skill \enSsNoRawMOne{} and the corrected skill \enSsNoCorMOne.

\subsection{At vehicle height, wind direction limits the drag estimate}
\label{sec:res-vehicle}
Against the van-mounted anemometer (Table~\ref{tab:vehicle}, Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}), the station-based estimate has a mean absolute speed error of \vehModel~m\,s$^{-1}$ over all \vehN{} segment passages, smaller than that of the nearest reference station (\vehRefraw) and the nearest road weather station (\vehRoadraw), but larger than that of the MET Nordic analysis (\vehMetnordic). The overall advantage over the nearest stations comes from Frøya, where both nearest stations are more than \vehFroyaKmMin~km away and represent opposite exposures: the road weather station is sheltered and the reference station exposed. At Dovre, where the road passes close to both a reference and a road weather station, the nearest road weather station is the best speed estimate and the model the least accurate one; most of its error arises on the evening of 31 October, when all estimates overestimate the van-measured wind (model \vehDovreEveModel\,\%, MET Nordic \vehDovreEveMetnordic\,\%, nearest road weather station \vehDovreEveRoadraw\,\%).

For the drag, the ranking is different. The median drag-force error of the corrected estimate (\vehDragModel\,\%) is smaller than that of the nearest stations (\vehDragRefraw{} and \vehDragRoadraw\,\%) and close to that of MET Nordic (\vehDragMetnordic\,\%). The remaining difference lies in the direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:vehicle}d): with the MET Nordic direction, the station-based speed gives \vehDragModelSpeedMnDir\,\%, and with the measured direction both speeds give the same error (\vehDragModelSpeedTrueDir{} and \vehDragMnSpeedTrueDir\,\%). The hourly air-drag energy along the drives shows the same picture (mean absolute error: no wind \enVehNone, nearest MET/Avinor station \enVehRef, nearest road weather station \enVehRoad, MET Nordic \enVehMn, corrected \enVehCor~kWh/100\,km; mean observed energy \enVehTrue).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Exposure decorrelates along the road over about 250\,m}{Exposure decorrelates along the road over about 250 m}}
\label{sec:res-along}
For vehicle studies the question is over which length a road segment has one exposure. The exposure features change quickly along the road (Fig.~\ref{fig:along}a): their correlation with the value at the station is \alongHundredMin--\alongHundredMax{} at \SI{100}{m}, \alongTwoFiftyMin--\alongTwoFiftyMax{} at \SI{250}{m} and \alongKmMin--\alongKmMax{} at \SI{1}{km} along the road, almost identically in both regions. Replacing the point features of the Swedish stations by averages over a road segment leaves the error unchanged for segments of \SI{200}{m} (\seSegTwo~$\pm$~\seSegTwoSE~m\,s$^{-1}$); segments of \SI{500}{m} and \SI{1}{km} tend to increase it, by about one standard error (Fig.~\ref{fig:along}b).

\subsection{The model form transfers, part of the coefficients does not}
\label{sec:res-transfer}
With the coefficients fitted in the Swedish check region, the median station error in Norway is \noWithSe~m\,s$^{-1}$ (own coefficients \noCvMed, raw \noCvRaw); conversely, the Norwegian coefficients give \seWithNo~m\,s$^{-1}$ in Sweden (own \seCvMed, raw \seCvRaw). The transferred model keeps only a small advantage in Norway, mainly because the Norwegian forest-fraction coefficient is \noFarRatio{} times the Swedish one. Even with its own coefficients the model fails at \noWorseN{} of \noTrv{} Norwegian stations (MAE raw $\rightarrow$ corrected in m\,s$^{-1}$): \noWorseList. These are a bridge, an exposed causeway over the sea, a mountain pass, a deep valley with strong channelling, and a station next to a reference station---situations that are rare in the training data or in which the nearest station is already representative.
